Game Physics & Feel.

I’m making a small platform game to see how changing a few numbers and rules can change the way a jump or dash feels.

What good game feel means to me

For me, a game feels good when the character does what I expect. A jump doesn’t have to work exactly like it would in real life. What matters is that it feels like the game understands what I’m trying to do.

Celeste got me interested in the little rules that make this happen. Coyote time lets you jump just after you’ve stepped off an edge. Input buffering remembers a jump button pressed just before landing. Corner correction helps you get past a corner when you’re only a little off. I like that the game gives you a bit of help with what you meant to do.

I also care about how a jump or dash looks and sounds. A short pause, a sound, or a trail behind the character can make a move feel much stronger. I want to try these things without making the screen so busy that it’s hard to play.

When I miss a jump, I want to try again right away. A quick restart means I still remember what went wrong and can do something different. That’s what I’m aiming for here: controls that feel quick, a little room for mistakes, and challenges that make me want one more try.

How the movement works

Jumping and dashing

Holding jump changes how high you go. Moving on the ground feels different from moving in the air. Dashes work in eight directions, with a bit of maths to keep diagonal dashes from being faster than the others.

Running into things

The code treats the player as a rectangle and checks when it touches a platform. It checks sideways movement and up-and-down movement separately. There are also platforms you can jump up through, and a little help getting past corners.

A little room for error

A jump can still count just after you leave an edge, or if you press jump a little before landing. I like how these small changes give the player some room to make mistakes.

Signal Forge

Signal Forge is the level editor. It lets me lay out rooms, change objects, undo mistakes, save or load a level, and try it out. A panel shows the player’s position, speed, and the boxes the game checks for contact. It helps me spot what’s going wrong.

In progress

There’s a version you can play, with movement, puzzles, a level editor, and tools to help find bugs. I’m still learning how the parts fit together and what needs to work better.

I want to check whether movement feels the same when the game runs faster or slower. I also need to stop the player from accidentally passing through thin walls at high speed and find a way to check the same problems again after changes. I’d like to add sloped platforms too.

I use Codex to help write code, find bugs, and keep notes. The GitHub notes explain how AI was used.
