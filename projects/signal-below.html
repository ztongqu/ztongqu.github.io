Game Physics & Feel.

A WORK IN PROGRESS

I’m making a small platform game to see how changing a few numbers and rules can change the way a jump or dash feels.

TypeScriptReactHTML CanvasMovement & collisions

What good game feel means to me

For me, good game feel means that the character responds in the way I expect. A jump does not have to follow real-world physics exactly to feel right. What matters is whether the game understands what I am trying to do.

Celeste made me interested in the small rules behind that feeling. Coyote time gives a player a brief chance to jump after leaving an edge. Input buffering remembers a jump pressed just before landing, while corner correction can help a character clear a slightly misjudged corner. These adjustments make me think about the difference between following an input literally and responding to the intention behind it.

I also want movement to feel clear and satisfying. Animation, sound, a short freeze, or a dash trail can help communicate the force of an action. I want to explore how much feedback is enough to make movement expressive without getting in the player's way.

Finally, a failed attempt should leave me wanting to try again. Quick restarts keep the previous attempt fresh in my mind, so I can adjust and learn. With this project, I hope to bring these ideas together: responsive controls, thoughtful forgiveness, and challenges that feel worth another attempt.

How the movement works

The movement and collision rules are in the project’s own code,
so I can look at them and change how they work.

01 / MOVEMENT

Jumping and dashing

Holding jump changes how high you go. Moving on the ground feels different from moving in the air. Dashes work in eight directions, with a bit of vector maths to stop diagonal ones from being faster.

02 / GEOMETRY

Running into things

The game checks whether the player’s rectangle overlaps a platform. It handles sideways and vertical movement separately, and includes one-way platforms and a little help around corners.

03 / INPUT

A little room for error

A jump can still count just after you leave an edge, or if you press jump a little before landing. I like how these small changes give the player some room to make mistakes.

Signal Forge & debugging tools

Signal Forge is the level editor. It lets me lay out rooms, change objects, undo mistakes, save or load a level, and try it out. The debugging panel shows things like position, speed, and collision boxes, which help me see what the game is doing.

In progress

What’s here so far

There’s a playable version with movement, puzzles, a level editor, and debugging tools. I’m still learning how the parts fit together and what needs to work better.

What I want to try next

I want to check whether movement stays consistent at different frame rates, stop fast movement from slipping through thin obstacles, and make tests I can run again. I’d also like to add sloped platforms.
